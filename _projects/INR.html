1. What is an INR?

An Implicit Neural Representation (INR) models a signal as a continuous function fθ: ℝd → ℝk, parameterized by a neural network. Instead of storing discrete samples (pixels, voxels, etc.), an INR learns a mapping from a coordinate (e.g. (x, y) in an image, (x, y, z) in a 3D volume) directly to the corresponding signal value (e.g. RGB intensity or occupancy).

Typical examples include:

  • 2D images as functions from spatial coordinates to RGB values.
  • 3D shapes as occupancy or signed distance fields.
  • Videos, audio, and other continuous signals represented in a coordinate domain.

Concretely, an INR is usually implemented as a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with a chosen activation function, trained to minimize the reconstruction error between predicted and ground-truth signal values at sampled coordinates.

We are interested in understanding implicit neural representations (INRs) with a mathemathical rigour by analyzing the role of activations in their spectral domain.

2. Why INRs?

INRs have emerged as powerful representations in vision, graphics, and signal processing due to several advantages:

  • Continuous & resolution-agnostic. Once trained, the network can be evaluated at arbitrary coordinates, enabling super-resolution and smooth interpolation without retraining.
  • Memory efficiency. Instead of storing large arrays, the signal is compressed into the network parameters.
  • Built-in regularization. The MLP architecture acts as a prior, often favoring smooth, structured reconstructions, which is beneficial for inverse problems.
  • Unified framework. The same INR backbone can be used for tasks like reconstruction, denoising, inpainting, and 3D field modeling by simply changing the loss and observation model.

However, the behavior of an INR is strongly influenced by its activation function, which controls how frequencies propagate and interact across layers.

3. Activations Play a Major Role in INRs

The activation function used in the MLP is central to the spectral behavior of an INR. When a coordinate input passes through nonlinear layers, harmonic distortion generates new frequency components, effectively “pushing” energy toward higher frequencies (often referred to as a blueshift).

Different activations induce different spectral profiles:

  • ReLU + positional encoding: relies on explicit high-frequency encodings; the MLP itself is relatively low-frequency.
  • Sinusoidal activations (e.g. SIREN): naturally support higher frequencies but may suffer from optimization instabilities and sensitivity to initialization.
  • Gaussian / windowed activations: can localize frequency content but may attenuate higher orders too quickly.

Understanding how an activation distributes spectral energy across polynomial bases (e.g. Chebyshev polynomials) offers a principled way to design INRs that are both expressive and stable.

4. Our Contribution

We study INRs through the lens of activation-induced spectra and propose an activation based on a band-shifted raised cosine filter, combined with a task-adaptive parameterization. Our main contributions are:

  • A Chebyshev-based analysis for dissecting the spectral loading of INR activations.
  • A band-limited, frequency-shifted activation with learnable bandwidth and shift, derived from a raised cosine filter.
  • A network architecture that conditions activation parameters on input-specific prior knowledge, leading to faster convergence and better reconstructions across tasks.

4.1 Dissecting Spectral Properties of INR Activations

We employ Chebyshev polynomial analysis to examine how different activations load energy into various spectral orders:

  • Expand each activation in a Chebyshev basis over a chosen interval and inspect the magnitude of coefficients across orders.
  • Compare raised cosine, sinusoidal, and Gaussian nonlinearities in terms of how long they maintain non-negligible coefficients.
  • Use chirp-image experiments to observe how energy spreads across frequencies and layers in practical INR training.

The analysis shows that a raised-cosine-based activation maintains stronger coefficients over a larger range of orders and yields more balanced high-frequency loading than purely sinusoidal or Gaussian activations.

4.2 Modifying the Network for Faster Convergence

Building on these findings, we design an INR architecture where the activation parameters are input-adaptive:

  • A truncated ResNet encoder (2D or 3D, depending on the task) extracts prior knowledge from the input signal.
  • A small harmonizer network transforms this latent representation into activation parameters (e.g., bandwidth T and shift ζ).
  • Parameters are constrained through a sigmoid projection to stay within stable, meaningful bounds.

This modification improves convergence speed and stability, and consistently boosts reconstruction quality over static-parameter baselines.

5. Results

5.1 Image Reconstruction (Kodak)

We evaluate on the Kodak dataset (24 lossless images) by representing each image as a continuous INR and measuring reconstruction quality:

  • Our activation achieves an average PSNR of 41.24 dB, compared to 35.57 dB for INCODE, and lower scores for ReLU+PE, SIREN, and WIRE.
  • On challenging images, the gain over prior work can exceed +8 dB.

Figure: Qualitative reconstruction on Kodak – our INR activation vs. existing baselines.

Figure: Radial profile / spectrum plot on Kodak showing improved high-frequency fidelity.

5.2 Image Denoising (DIV2K)

For denoising, the INR is used as a deep image prior under a physically motivated noise model:

  • Photon noise (Poisson, mean count 30) combined with Gaussian readout noise (standard deviation 2).
  • Our method improves PSNR by about +0.46 dB over INCODE, with better structural preservation and reduced color artifacts.

Figure: Denoising on DIV2K – INR with band-shifted raised cosine vs. other INR activations.

5.3 Single-Image Super-Resolution

We test 2×, 4×, and 6× super-resolution on DIV2K by training the INR on low-resolution observations and querying it at higher-resolution grids:

Method 2× PSNR 4× PSNR 6× PSNR

INCODE32.8329.9626.63

Qualitatively, the INR with our activation reconstructs sharper edges and fine details (e.g., textures and thin structures) with fewer ringing artifacts, especially at 6×.

Figure: Super-resolution examples at 2×, 4×, and 6× – our INR activation recovers high-frequency content more faithfully.

5.4 Inpainting

For inpainting, only a subset of pixels is observed and the INR is optimized to match them while implicitly filling missing regions:

  • On a Celtic spiral knots image (572×582) with 20% pixel visibility, our INR more reliably recovers continuous knot structures and fine curves.
  • PSNR and SSIM are competitive or superior to existing INR activations, particularly on sparsely observed patterns.

Figure: Inpainting with 20% observations – INR with band-shifted raised cosine reconstructs sparse, structured patterns.

Other Results

Beyond 2D imaging tasks, the same INR design extends to 3D fields:

  • 3D occupancy fields: reconstruct volumetric shapes (e.g., a statue on a 512³ grid) with high IOU and clean surfaces.
  • Ablations: width–depth experiments show a clear trade-off between capacity and PSNR, with our activation benefiting more from increased model capacity than baselines.

Figure: 3D occupancy reconstruction and width–depth ablations demonstrating the scalability of the proposed INR.
